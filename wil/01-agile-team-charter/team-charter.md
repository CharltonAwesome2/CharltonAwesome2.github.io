# Agile Team Charter
## Individual Developer Working Charter

**Work period:** 1 January 2026 - 31 December 2026  
**Working arrangement:** Individual developer working with a supervisor and project stakeholders  
**Development approach:** Agile-informed, iterative development  
**Team structure:** Individual contributor

---

## 1. Purpose

This charter describes the working principles, responsibilities, communication arrangements and development practices applied during the 2026 work-integrated learning period.

The work involved several software development engagements with different requirements, stakeholders, priorities and completion states. Development was primarily undertaken independently, with requirements and feedback communicated through a supervisor and, where applicable, other project stakeholders.

The purpose of this charter is to document how development work was organised and managed, how responsibilities were handled, how changing requirements were approached and how quality was considered throughout development.

This is a retrospective working charter. It documents the practical working arrangement and Agile-informed principles applied during the period. It does not imply that a formal Scrum team, formal sprint ceremonies or an established Agile framework were used.

## 2. Working Arrangement and Team Structure

Development was performed by one individual developer rather than a conventional Agile development team.

The working arrangement involved three principal roles:

| Role | Responsibilities |
|---|---|
| Developer | Analyse requirements, conduct research, design and implement solutions, test functionality, resolve defects, maintain source code and communicate progress or technical concerns. |
| Supervisor | Communicate project requirements and priorities, provide direction and feedback, and act as the primary point of communication between the developer and relevant stakeholders. |
| Project stakeholder | Provide project requirements, clarify business needs, review deliverables and provide feedback or decisions where applicable. |

The developer was responsible for the practical implementation of assigned development work. Requirements and feedback were generally communicated by the supervisor. The developer then interpreted those requirements, investigated possible solutions and implemented the necessary changes.

Stakeholder involvement varied between engagements. In some cases, direct stakeholder discussions took place, while in others, communication was handled through the supervisor.

Because the developer worked independently, responsibilities normally distributed across a development team were handled by the developer. These included technical investigation, implementation, testing, troubleshooting and maintaining the codebase.

## 3. Project Scope

The 2026 working period involved four principal professional software development engagements.

For confidentiality, these engagements are referred to throughout this portfolio as Project A, Project B, Project C and Project D.

| Project reference | General scope | Development status |
|---|---|---|
| Project A | Development of a web-based commercial application with catalogue, search and supporting application functionality. | Development discontinued following a stakeholder decision. |
| Project B | Development of a corporate web presence with multiple organisational sections. | Completed, deployed and accepted. |
| Project C | Development of a web-based information management application supporting organisational and administrative processes. | Development paused while awaiting feedback. |
| Project D | Development of a computer-vision application involving camera-based identification and tracking functionality. | Development paused. |

The projects had different objectives, technical requirements and development constraints. Consequently, the work completed and the meaning of project completion varied between engagements.

Academic projects undertaken as part of university studies are not included as professional engagements in this charter. They are recognised as competing commitments that affected the time available for professional development work.

## 4. Working Principles

The following principles describe the approach used to manage and complete development work.

### 4.1 Understand requirements before implementation

Requirements should be reviewed and understood before implementation begins. Where necessary, research should be conducted to understand the problem, identify possible solutions and determine an appropriate technical approach.

Where requirements are unclear, clarification should be sought rather than relying entirely on assumptions.

### 4.2 Deliver work incrementally

Large development tasks should be approached in manageable sections. Related functionality should be implemented and reviewed progressively rather than treating the entire project as one development task.

This makes it easier to identify defects, review implementation decisions and accommodate changes.

### 4.3 Maintain flexibility

Project priorities and requirements may change. Development work should therefore remain flexible enough to accommodate new priorities, revised requirements and stakeholder feedback.

Changes should be assessed before implementation so that their effect on existing functionality can be understood.

### 4.4 Prioritise maintainability

Solutions should be designed with readability, modularity, maintainability and future development in mind.

Where possible, existing functionality should be reused or extended instead of unnecessarily duplicating code. Research into established solutions and development practices should inform technical decisions.

### 4.5 Preserve development progress

Source code should be maintained using version control. Changes should be committed regularly enough to preserve meaningful development progress and provide a record of implementation activity.

Existing functionality should be considered when introducing changes, particularly where new requirements may affect previously implemented features.

### 4.6 Communicate progress and concerns

Requirements, changes, uncertainties and technical concerns should be communicated to the supervisor or relevant stakeholder.

Where a requirement cannot be implemented as originally expected, the issue and its possible implications should be raised rather than silently changing the intended functionality.

## 5. Responsibilities

### Developer responsibilities

The developer was responsible for:

- Reviewing and interpreting requirements.
- Conducting technical research where necessary.
- Identifying suitable technologies, libraries and implementation approaches.
- Designing application structures and database relationships where required.
- Implementing application functionality.
- Integrating application components and services.
- Testing implemented functionality and investigating defects.
- Maintaining and updating source code.
- Reviewing existing functionality when requirements changed.
- Using version control to preserve and track development changes.
- Communicating progress, technical difficulties and implementation concerns.
- Applying feedback and making appropriate revisions.

### Supervisor responsibilities

The supervisor was generally responsible for:

- Communicating development requirements.
- Providing project direction and priorities.
- Clarifying requirements where necessary.
- Communicating feedback received from stakeholders.
- Providing guidance when project priorities or requirements changed.

### Stakeholder responsibilities

Depending on the engagement, stakeholders were responsible for:

- Identifying business or operational requirements.
- Providing relevant information about the intended application.
- Reviewing work or proposed changes.
- Providing feedback and communicating decisions.
- Confirming whether a deliverable met the relevant expectations.

## 6. Communication and Collaboration

Communication was primarily conducted through the supervisor, who generally communicated requirements and feedback to the developer.

Communication methods included verbal discussions and email correspondence where applicable. Stakeholder communication arrangements differed between engagements.

The developer was expected to:

1. Review the requirements communicated by the supervisor.
2. Identify uncertainties or technical considerations.
3. Request clarification when necessary.
4. Implement the agreed functionality.
5. Communicate relevant progress, issues or changes.
6. Apply feedback where required.

Formal daily stand-ups, sprint review meetings, sprint retrospectives and other recurring Scrum ceremonies were not part of the established working arrangement.

The absence of these ceremonies did not prevent communication or iterative development, but it meant that progress tracking and prioritisation were managed more informally.

## 7. Work Prioritisation

Work was distributed across projects according to the priorities communicated and the practical demands of each engagement.

Prioritisation was flexible rather than governed by a formal backlog or fixed sprint schedule.

The general approach considered:

- The importance and urgency of newly communicated work.
- The size and complexity of the new requirement.
- Whether the current task or project was close to a useful milestone.
- Whether completing the current task would provide a more practical stopping point.
- Whether the new priority required work to be switched immediately.

Work allocation often followed a FILO tendency, but this was not treated as an absolute rule. The importance and size of incoming work could justify finishing the current task, completing a milestone or temporarily switching to another project.

This approach allowed multiple engagements to progress according to changing priorities, although it also required careful consideration of unfinished work when switching between projects.

## 8. Development Workflow

The following workflow represents the general iterative approach used during development.

1. **Requirement review:** Review the requirements communicated by the supervisor or stakeholder.
2. **Analysis and research:** Investigate the problem, identify dependencies and research possible solutions.
3. **Planning and design:** Determine the implementation approach, relevant components and any required data structures or relationships.
4. **Implementation:** Develop the required functionality in manageable sections.
5. **Testing:** Test the implemented functionality and investigate unexpected behaviour.
6. **Revision:** Correct defects, improve the implementation and incorporate feedback or revised requirements.
7. **Version control:** Commit relevant changes to preserve progress and maintain a development history.
8. **Review or handover:** Where applicable, make the completed work available for stakeholder review, deployment or further development.

The workflow was iterative. A requirement could return to analysis or design if testing, research or feedback revealed that a different implementation approach was necessary.

## 9. Managing Changing Requirements

Requirements could be revised as projects developed or as stakeholders provided additional feedback.

The general approach to handling changes was:

1. Preserve the current work through source control and relevant backups.
2. Review what had already been implemented.
3. Analyse the new requirement and its effect on existing functionality.
4. Identify related functionality that might also need to be updated.
5. Determine whether existing components could be extended or whether new modular functionality was required.
6. Implement the changes.
7. Test the affected functionality and check for unintended effects.

Where a change affected multiple areas of an application, related functions and components were reviewed to maintain consistency.

This approach helped reduce the risk of introducing changes that solved one problem while creating inconsistencies elsewhere.

## 10. Quality and Definition of Done

Quality was considered throughout development rather than only at the end of a project.

The developer aimed to ensure that implemented functionality:

- Addressed the communicated requirement.
- Worked as intended under the tested conditions.
- Integrated appropriately with related application components.
- Did not unnecessarily disrupt existing functionality.
- Was reasonably readable and maintainable.
- Had relevant defects investigated and corrected.
- Was committed to version control.

For work submitted for stakeholder review, the relevant deliverable also needed to be made available for review.

The meaning of completion differed between projects. A completed feature did not necessarily mean that the entire project was complete. Some deliverables were completed and accepted, while other engagements were paused or discontinued with functionality still outstanding.

No single formal Definition of Done was maintained across all four engagements. The criteria above describe the practical quality considerations applied during development and provide a more consistent standard for future work.

## 11. Tools and Development Resources

The development work used tools and technologies appropriate to the individual engagements.

These included:

- **Git and version-control hosting:** Source control, code history and collaboration where applicable.
- **Development environments and code editors:** Application development, debugging and maintenance.
- **Project-specific frameworks and libraries:** Selected according to the requirements of each application.
- **Databases:** Data modelling, storage and retrieval where required.
- **Testing and debugging tools:** Verification of implemented functionality and investigation of defects.
- **Email and verbal communication:** Requirements, feedback and project coordination.

The specific technologies differed between engagements. This charter therefore does not prescribe one technology stack for all development work.

## 12. Documentation and Knowledge Management

Technical research and investigation formed an important part of development, particularly when designing systems or implementing functionality without an existing implementation to follow.

Source code and version-control history provided records of implementation activity. However, documentation of research, technical decisions, requirements and progress was not consistently maintained in a centralised format.

Improving documentation is an identified area for future development work.

Future projects should maintain more structured records of:

- Requirements and clarifications.
- Important technical decisions and the reasons for them.
- Research resources and useful references.
- Implementation progress.
- Known limitations and outstanding work.
- Testing results and stakeholder feedback.

This would make it easier to resume work, understand previous decisions and maintain consistency when requirements change.

## 13. Limitations of the Working Arrangement

The individual working arrangement provided flexibility and allowed development decisions to be made directly during implementation. However, it also introduced limitations.

These included:

- A significant range of responsibilities being handled by one developer.
- Limited formal documentation of requirements and decisions.
- Informal progress tracking.
- Variable time available due to competing professional and academic commitments.
- The possibility of losing context when switching between projects.
- Limited opportunities for regular peer review within the development process.

These limitations are relevant to understanding how the work was managed and identify areas that could be improved through more structured Agile practices.

## 14. Future Improvements

The following improvements would strengthen the working arrangement in future projects:

1. Maintain a documented product backlog containing requirements, priorities and task status.
2. Break larger requirements into smaller, clearly defined tasks.
3. Record progress consistently, including completed work, outstanding tasks and blockers.
4. Maintain a technical decision log and record useful research resources.
5. Clarify requirements and priorities before beginning substantial implementation.
6. Communicate progress and potential delays more consistently.
7. Establish a practical review process for completed features.
8. Define completion criteria for individual tasks and project milestones.
9. Use lightweight iteration planning to make project switching and prioritisation more predictable.
10. Improve commit messages so that version-control history communicates the purpose and scope of changes clearly.

## 15. Charter Summary

This charter describes an individual development arrangement based on independent implementation, supervisor-directed requirements, stakeholder feedback and iterative development.

Although formal Scrum practices were not followed, several principles associated with Agile development were relevant to the work: responding to changing requirements, delivering functionality incrementally, incorporating feedback, maintaining working software and adapting priorities.

The main opportunity for improvement is to introduce more consistent planning, documentation, progress tracking and communication without losing the flexibility required when working across several projects.
