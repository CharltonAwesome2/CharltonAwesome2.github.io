# Reflection: Agile Team Charter

## 1. Working Independently

During the 2026 work period, I primarily worked as an individual developer rather than as part of a conventional software development team. Requirements were generally communicated to me by my supervisor, after which I was responsible for analysing the requirements, researching possible solutions and implementing the necessary functionality.

Working independently gave me considerable responsibility for technical decisions and implementation. It also meant that I had to manage several responsibilities that would normally be distributed across different members of a development team.

This arrangement helped me develop greater independence and confidence in approaching unfamiliar problems. However, it also highlighted the importance of having clear requirements, maintaining documentation and communicating progress. When working independently, it is particularly important to keep track of technical decisions and unfinished work because there are fewer opportunities for another team member to provide context or identify an issue.

## 2. Applying Agile Principles

Although I did not follow a formal Agile framework or conduct formal Scrum ceremonies, aspects of my working approach were consistent with Agile principles.

Development was iterative. Functionality was implemented progressively, tested and revised where necessary. Requirements and feedback could result in changes to existing functionality or the introduction of additional components.

I also had to adapt to changing project priorities. Work was not always completed in a single uninterrupted sequence, and I sometimes had to switch between projects or complete a milestone before moving to another priority.

This experience demonstrated that flexibility is important, but flexibility should be supported by sufficient planning and documentation. Without a clear record of outstanding tasks and previous decisions, switching between projects can make it more difficult to resume work efficiently.

## 3. Communication and Requirements

Communication was generally handled through my supervisor, who communicated requirements and feedback. This arrangement allowed me to focus on implementation, but it also meant that I sometimes had to interpret requirements independently.

One of the challenges was designing systems from the ground up. Without detailed documentation covering every requirement or implementation decision, I had to research how systems should work and determine suitable approaches.

This was valuable for developing my problem-solving skills, but it also showed me that asking questions before implementation can prevent unnecessary rework. In future projects, I would aim to clarify requirements more thoroughly, especially where a decision could affect database design, application architecture or future functionality.

I would also communicate progress more consistently so that the supervisor and relevant stakeholders have a clearer understanding of what has been completed, what remains outstanding and whether any decisions are required.

## 4. Managing Priorities

Working across multiple projects required me to make decisions about which work should receive attention first.

My approach was flexible. I considered the importance and size of incoming work, the progress already made on the current task and whether completing a milestone would provide a sensible point at which to switch projects.

This helped me respond to changing priorities, but it was not always supported by a formal task-tracking system. A documented backlog and more consistent progress records would make this process more transparent and reduce the possibility of losing track of unfinished work.

## 5. Technical Growth and Quality

One of my most important lessons was that producing working code and producing maintainable code are not necessarily the same thing.

As I gained experience and researched different implementation approaches, I sometimes identified shortcomings in earlier versions of my work. This led me to revisit existing functionality, improve the structure of code and consider backward compatibility when introducing changes.

I learned that it is not always necessary or beneficial to create a completely new solution. In many cases, established solutions and recognised development practices already address the problem more effectively.

Researching how experienced developers have approached similar problems can help avoid unnecessary reinvention and improve the quality of implementation.

I also learned that maintainability, modularity and consistency should be considered from the beginning rather than treated as improvements that can always be postponed.

## 6. What I Would Improve

If I were to approach the same working arrangement again, I would make several changes.

First, I would maintain more comprehensive documentation from the beginning of each project. This would include requirements, technical decisions, research resources and outstanding tasks.

Second, I would establish a reusable project structure or starter template for common development requirements. This would reduce the amount of repetitive setup work and allow more time to be spent on project-specific functionality.

Third, I would ask more questions before beginning substantial implementation. Clarifying requirements and priorities early could reduce misunderstandings and unnecessary rework.

Finally, I would communicate progress more consistently and use a more structured task-management approach. Even when working independently, maintaining a prioritised backlog and recording completed work would make project management more predictable.

## 7. Overall Reflection

Working independently across several development engagements strengthened my ability to research, analyse requirements, make technical decisions and implement software solutions.

It also showed me that technical ability alone is not sufficient for effective software development. Planning, communication, documentation, prioritisation and maintainability are equally important.

The experience helped me understand the practical value of Agile principles, particularly iterative development, responding to change and incorporating feedback. It also showed me where a more structured approach could improve my effectiveness.

My main takeaway is that **coding is easy; coding well is difficult**. Developing a solution is only part of the responsibility. The solution must also be understandable, maintainable, appropriate to the requirements and capable of accommodating future changes.
